Day 4, Morning • Domain 6

Security Assessment and Testing

A control that has never been tested is a hope, not a control. Domain 6 asks how you know your security works: which test fits which question, who should run it, what the results mean, and how leaders and outside parties gain assurance. The test is only half the work. The report and the follow-through are the rest.

Session: Day 4, 9:00 to 12:00 Duration: ~3 hrs Exam Weight: 11.2% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Prove it, then report it

Assessment and testing turn assumptions into evidence. A vulnerability scan finds known weaknesses. A penetration test shows what an attacker could actually do. A code review finds flaws before they ship. An audit tells a third party whether controls are designed well and operate over time. Each answers a different question, so the first step is always to ask what you need to know.

This lesson covers assessment strategy, the main control testing techniques, the security process data that management reviews, how to analyze and report results, and how internal, external, and third-party audits work, including SOC reports.

🧭 Think like a managerTesting needs written authorization, a defined scope, and an owner for the results. When an answer jumps straight to running a tool, look for the option that first confirms approval and scope, or that routes findings to the people who can fix them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Design an assessment strategy that names the scope, the assessor, and the location of the systems. Bloom: Create
  • Select the testing technique that best answers a stated question under real constraints. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Distinguish SAST, DAST, fuzzing, code review, vulnerability assessment, penetration testing, and attack simulations. Bloom: Analyze
  • Interpret key performance and key risk indicators drawn from security process data. Bloom: Analyze
  • Compare SOC 1, SOC 2 Type I, SOC 2 Type II, and SOC 3 reports by audience and content. Bloom: Understand
  • Build a student assessment exercise that ends in a remediation report. Bloom: Create

Assessment Strategy

A strategy decides what to test, why, how often, and who does it. It starts from risk and requirements: a system that handles payment data or student records gets more frequent and deeper testing than a public brochure site. The strategy is then validated, which means confirming that the planned tests actually cover the risks and the requirements.

Who performs the assessment

AssessorWho it isStrengthLimit
InternalStaff within the organization’s controlKnows the systems; low cost; frequentLess independent; may share blind spots
ExternalAn outside firm hired by the organizationIndependent view and specialist skillCost; needs time to learn the environment
Third-partyAn independent party outside the enterprise, often reporting to someone else, such as a customer or regulatorHighest independence; trusted by outsidersLeast control over scope and timing

Location matters as much as the assessor. On-premises systems can be tested directly. Cloud systems are governed by the provider’s terms, and many providers limit or require notice for some tests. Hybrid environments need both, plus testing of the connections between them. In cloud services the customer often relies on the provider’s third-party audit reports for the layers it cannot test.

💡 Exam TipBefore testing a cloud-hosted system, check the provider’s acceptable use and testing policy. Authorization from your own organization is necessary but not always sufficient, because the provider owns the underlying infrastructure.

Vulnerability and Penetration Testing

A vulnerability assessment is wide and shallow. Scanners compare systems against databases of known weaknesses, such as CVE entries, and rate them, often with CVSS scores. Credentialed (authenticated) scans log in and see far more than uncredentialed scans, which see only what an outsider sees. Scanners also produce false positives, so results need validation.

A penetration test is narrow and deep. Testers try to exploit weaknesses and chain them together to show business impact. Typical phases are planning, discovery (reconnaissance and scanning), attack (gain access, escalate, move), and reporting. Written authorization and rules of engagement come first.

Knowledge levels and exercise types

  • Black box (zero knowledge): testers start with no inside information, as an outside attacker would. Realistic but slow.
  • Gray box (partial knowledge): testers get some information, such as user credentials or a network map. A common balance of realism and efficiency.
  • White box (full knowledge): testers get documentation and often code. The most thorough coverage for the time spent.
  • Team exercises: a red team plays the adversary, a blue team defends, and a purple team exercise has both working together so every attack becomes a detection lesson. The labels are common in practice even where an exam item does not use them.

Attack simulations

An attack simulation reproduces adversary behavior on purpose to see whether defenses hold. It differs from a penetration test in its question. A penetration test asks what an attacker could reach. An attack simulation asks whether our controls detect, block, and respond to a specific technique, and whether they still do after the last change.

  • Breach and attack simulation (BAS) tools: automated agents run safe versions of known techniques, often mapped to MITRE ATT&CK, on a schedule. They report which steps were blocked, which were detected, and which went unseen.
  • Adversary emulation: a team follows the documented playbook of a specific threat group from start to finish, so defenders practice against a realistic chain rather than isolated steps.
  • Tabletop and scenario exercises: people walk through an attack narrative to test decisions, escalation, and communication when no tool is involved.

Simulations need the same authorization and rules of engagement as any test. Safe payloads, change windows, and a stop condition keep a simulation from becoming an incident.

Vulnerability assessmentAnswers “what known weaknesses exist?” Automated, frequent, broad. Does not prove exploitability.
Penetration testAnswers “what could an attacker actually do?” Manual and tool-assisted, periodic, deep. Shows impact and attack paths.
💡 Exam TipThe most important document for a penetration test is written authorization from someone with the authority to grant it. Without it, the same activity can be a crime.

Testing Software and Interfaces

Code review and testing find flaws in software before attackers do. The exam expects you to know what each technique needs and what it finds.

TechniqueNeedsFinds best
SASTSource code or binaries; no running appCoding flaws early, such as injection patterns and unsafe functions
Manual code reviewSource code and skilled reviewersLogic and design flaws that tools miss; the slowest option
DASTA running applicationRuntime and configuration flaws visible from outside
FuzzingA running program or test harnessCrashes, memory errors, and input handling failures

Some teams add instrumentation agents inside the running application so dynamic findings point to the exact line of code. Treat this as a refinement of dynamic testing. The core exam contrast is static versus dynamic: code at rest versus a system in motion.

  • Misuse case testing: write how a feature could be abused, then test that the system resists it.
  • Coverage analysis: measures how much of the code or requirements the tests exercised, for example statement, branch, or condition coverage. High coverage does not prove security, but low coverage proves gaps.
  • Interface testing: checks the points where components meet. User interfaces, network interfaces, and application programming interfaces (APIs) each need tests for authentication, input validation, and error handling.
  • Synthetic transactions and benchmarks: scripted checks that a live service works and meets performance targets. Real user monitoring, by contrast, watches actual traffic passively.
  • Log reviews: confirm that events are captured, protected, and actually examined.
  • Compliance checks: compare configurations against a baseline or standard, such as a CIS Benchmark.
💡 Exam Tip“Earliest in the life cycle” and “cheapest to fix” point to SAST and code review. “Running application” and “no source code” point to DAST. “Proves real impact” points to a penetration test.
4

Security Process Data

Tests are periodic. Process data is continuous. Domain 6 expects you to collect technical and administrative data that shows whether security processes work, and to present it to management in a form they can act on.

  • Account management: orphaned accounts, accounts not reviewed on schedule, and time to remove access after termination.
  • Management review and approval: evidence that leaders review results, accept residual risk, and approve exceptions.
  • Key performance indicators (KPIs): how well a process performs, such as percent of systems patched within 30 days.
  • Key risk indicators (KRIs): early warning that risk is rising, such as the count of critical findings past due.
  • Backup verification data: results of restore tests, not just successful backup jobs.
  • Training and awareness: phishing simulation click and report rates.
  • Disaster recovery and business continuity: test dates, results, and whether recovery met the RTO.
KPI: looks back“We patched 94% of critical vulnerabilities within 15 days last quarter.” It measures performance against a target.
KRI: looks ahead“Twelve internet-facing servers run an operating system that loses vendor support in 60 days.” It signals risk before an incident.
💡 Exam TipA backup job that reports “success” proves only that data was written. A test restore proves it can be recovered. When asked for the BEST evidence of backup effectiveness, choose the restore test.
5

Analysis, Reporting, and Audits

Raw test output is not a report. Analysis removes false positives, rates findings by risk to the business, and groups related issues by root cause. The report then goes to the right audiences: a short executive summary for leaders and detailed findings with steps to reproduce for technical teams.

  • Remediation: each finding gets an owner, a deadline based on severity, and a retest to confirm the fix.
  • Exception handling: when a finding cannot be fixed, the risk owner documents a formal exception with compensating controls and an expiration date.

Responsible disclosure

When a test, an audit, or plain curiosity uncovers a flaw in someone else’s product, the finder owes a duty to the users of that product. Responsible disclosure, also called coordinated disclosure, balances two risks: telling attackers too soon, and leaving users exposed too long.

  • Report privately first: contact the vendor through its published security contact or vulnerability disclosure policy, with enough detail to reproduce the issue.
  • Agree on a timeline: many programs use about 90 days before public release, shortened if the flaw is already being exploited and extended if a fix is in progress.
  • Use a coordinator when needed: if the vendor does not respond, a coordinator such as CERT/CC can help. A CVE identifier is assigned through a CVE Numbering Authority so everyone tracks the same flaw.
  • Bug bounty programs: some vendors invite testing within a published scope and pay for valid reports. The scope and safe harbor terms are the authorization; testing outside them is not covered.
  • Receive reports well: an organization should publish its own disclosure policy and a contact, such as a security.txt file, so finders know how to reach it.

Analyzing audit output

Reporting covers audit results as well as test results. An audit report lists control deficiencies and exceptions, each with a management response: the corrective action, the owner, and the date. Track those responses like test findings, and confirm the fix at the next audit. When you rely on a vendor’s SOC report, read the exceptions and the complementary user entity controls, the controls the customer must operate for the vendor’s controls to work.

Audits and SOC reports

An audit checks controls against a standard and gives assurance to someone who cannot check for themselves. Auditors must be independent of the area they audit. Service organizations commonly provide System and Organization Controls (SOC) reports, issued under the AICPA’s SSAE 18 attestation standard. ISAE 3402 is the international counterpart for controls relevant to financial reporting.

ReportFocusAudience
SOC 1Controls relevant to the customer’s financial reportingCustomers’ auditors and finance; restricted use
SOC 2 Type ISecurity and related trust services criteria; design at a point in timeCustomers and partners under NDA; restricted use
SOC 2 Type IISame criteria; design and operating effectiveness over a period, often 6 to 12 monthsCustomers and partners under NDA; restricted use
SOC 3A short summary of the SOC 2 opinionGeneral public; often posted on a website
💡 Exam TipTo learn whether a cloud provider’s security controls actually worked over the past year, ask for a SOC 2 Type II. A Type I shows only that controls were designed well on one day.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Students often treat testing as running tools. Domain 6 teaches them that the question comes first and the report comes last, and that both matter as much as the scan.

    • Signature assignment: give teams a fictional college system, a scope statement, and simulated scan output with planted false positives. Teams write an assessment plan, validate the findings, and deliver a two-audience report: a one-page executive summary and a technical findings table with owners and deadlines.
    • Rubric criteria: technique choices justified by the stated question, false positives correctly removed, findings ranked by business risk, remediation with owners and retest dates, and a clean executive summary with no jargon.
    • Lab idea: run a credentialed and an uncredentialed scan of the same isolated lab host and compare the results. Then have students read a real, publicly posted SOC 3 report and list what it does and does not tell a customer.
    • Course fit: this domain maps to vulnerability assessment, ethical hacking, and audit or compliance courses. It supports the CAE-CD Vulnerability Analysis (VLA) and IA Compliance (IAC) Knowledge Units.
    🎓 Teaching noteUse the challenge mode as a warm-up, then ask students to write a seventh scenario where the BEST answer is log review. Writing a good distractor forces them to understand why the other techniques do not fit.

    Scored Knowledge Check

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.A college hires a firm to run a penetration test of its admissions portal. What must be in place BEFORE testing begins?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: B. Written authorization and agreed rules of engagement come first. They define scope, timing, and limits, and they separate a legal test from an attack. The other items may help, but none is required to start.

    Q2.Testers receive user credentials and a network diagram, but no source code or design documents. What type of test is this?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: D. Partial knowledge is gray box. Black box gives no inside information, and white box gives full knowledge, often including code. Double blind describes whether the defenders know the test is happening.

    Q3.A development team wants to find security flaws as early as possible, before any part of the application can run. Which technique is MOST appropriate?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: A. SAST analyzes code without executing it, so it works before anything runs. DAST, synthetic transactions, and fuzzing all need a running program.

    Q4.A customer needs assurance that a payroll provider’s security controls operated effectively over the last twelve months. Which report BEST meets the need?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: C. A SOC 2 Type II covers both design and operating effectiveness of security controls over a period. Type I covers design at a point in time, SOC 3 is a brief public summary, and internal scans are not independent.

    Q5.A security operations manager wants to confirm every week, without disrupting users, that the endpoint and email controls still detect and block a set of known ransomware techniques. Which approach BEST meets this goal?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: C. Attack simulations replay known techniques safely and report whether controls blocked, detected, or missed each one, so they fit frequent control validation. A penetration test is too infrequent, a scan finds weaknesses rather than testing detection, and a SOC report describes the provider’s controls, not yours.

    Q6.An administrator says backups are effective because every nightly job reported success for six months. What is the BEST evidence to request?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: A. Successful jobs show that data was written, not that it can be recovered. Test restores are the backup verification data that proves recoverability.

    Q7.A penetration test finds a flaw that the business owner cannot fix for nine months because of a vendor dependency. What is the MOST appropriate action?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: D. Exception handling records the risk, the compensating controls, the approving owner, and when the exception ends. Security staff advise but do not accept risk for the business, and hiding findings defeats the purpose of testing.

    Q8.A faculty member reported a serious flaw in a commercial learning platform to the vendor’s security contact six weeks ago. The vendor has not replied. What is the BEST next step?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: B. Responsible, or coordinated, disclosure does not end when a vendor is silent. A coordinator can reach the vendor and set a public timeline that protects users without arming attackers early. Testing other colleges’ systems without authorization is illegal, and dropping the matter fails the duty to protect society in the ISC2 Code.
