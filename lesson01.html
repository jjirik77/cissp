Day 1, Late Morning and Afternoon • Domain 1

Security Governance and Risk Management

Domain 1 ties for the largest share of the exam and is the lens for every other domain. It asks a manager’s questions: what does the business need, what does the law require, how much risk is acceptable, and who owns the decision. The technology comes later. The judgment comes first.

Session: Day 1, 10:30 to 5:00 Duration: ~5 hrs Exam Weight: 13.6% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Security serves the business

Domain 1 sets the tone for the whole exam. Security exists to protect the mission, not to win arguments about technology. A CISSP aligns security with business goals, follows the law, writes policy that people can follow, measures risk in terms leaders understand, and recommends a response. Senior management then accepts the risk and owns the outcome.

This lesson covers governance, law and compliance, the ethical use of artificial intelligence, policy and personnel security, risk management, business continuity requirements, supply chain risk, awareness programs, and threat modeling. It is the longest session of the week because it ties for the most weight and frames every domain that follows.

🧭 Think like a managerWhen two answers both reduce risk, the better answer usually starts with governance: understand the business need, follow policy, and involve the right owner. Fixing it yourself, right now, is rarely the CISSP answer.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Explain how governance aligns security with strategy, and distinguish due care from due diligence. Bloom: Understand
  • Classify legal, regulatory, and investigation requirements by type and burden of proof. Bloom: Analyze

  • Apply STRIDE to the elements of a data flow diagram and choose mitigations. Bloom: Apply

Security Concepts and Governance

The exam builds on five security properties. Confidentiality keeps information from people who should not see it. Integrity keeps it accurate and unaltered. Availability keeps it usable when it is needed. Authenticity confirms that a person, system, or message is genuine. Nonrepudiation prevents a sender from denying an action they took. ISC2 calls these the five pillars of information security.

Governance is how leadership directs and oversees security. It sets the goals and the risk appetite, assigns accountability, and checks results. Management then runs the program within those boundaries.

The exam outline asks you to evaluate, apply, and sustain governance. Building a program is the easy part. Keeping it healthy means reviewing the strategy as the business changes, refreshing policy on a schedule, reporting metrics to the board, renewing executive sponsorship, and fixing the gaps that audits and incidents reveal.

What governance covers

  • Alignment with strategy: the security program supports the mission, goals, and objectives. A strategic plan covers about five years, a tactical plan about one year, and operational plans cover weeks to months.
  • Organizational processes: acquisitions bring in unknown systems and risk, so assess them before they connect. Divestitures require removing access and data cleanly. Governance committees, such as a steering committee, approve priorities and budgets.
  • Roles and responsibilities: senior management holds final accountability. The CISO runs the program. Data owners classify data and approve access. Custodians operate the controls. Users follow policy. Auditors provide independent assurance.
  • Control frameworks: ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002 (an information security management system and its controls), the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (six functions: Govern, which version 2.0 added, plus Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover) and SP 800-53, COBIT (governance of enterprise IT), SABSA (business-driven security architecture), PCI DSS (payment card data), and FedRAMP (cloud services for the U.S. federal government).
Due careActing on what you know. The organization implements and operates reasonable controls. Example: the college patches its servers and enforces MFA after a risk assessment identifies the need.
Due diligenceMaking sure you know. The organization researches, assesses, and verifies. Example: the college reviews a cloud vendor’s SOC 2 report before signing, then monitors the vendor each year.
💡 Exam TipA memory aid many instructors use: due diligence is thinking before and after you act, and due care is doing. Failing at either can support a negligence claim. Accountability for both sits with senior management, even when tasks are delegated.

Law, AI Ethics, and Investigations

A CISSP does not practice law, but must recognize legal requirements and bring in counsel. The exam tests categories and consequences more than the details of any single statute.

Legal and regulatory issues

  • Cybercrimes and data breaches: computer crime laws, such as the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, and breach notification laws that set deadlines for telling regulators and affected people.
  • Licensing and intellectual property: copyright protects expression, patents protect inventions, trademarks protect brand identity, and trade secrets protect confidential business information for as long as it stays secret. Software licenses limit how software may be used.
  • Import and export controls: some encryption and dual-use technology needs a license to export. In the U.S., ITAR covers defense items and the EAR covers commercial and dual-use items.
  • Transborder data flow: personal data moving between countries must meet the rules of both. Data localization laws may require some data to stay in country.
  • Privacy: the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the California Consumer Privacy Act, China’s Personal Information Protection Law, South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act, and the U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), which protects health information held by covered entities such as health plans and providers and by their business associates. Each grants rights to individuals and duties to the organizations that collect data.
  • Financial and corporate laws: in the U.S., the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) requires financial institutions, including insurers and lenders, to protect customer financial information and explain how they share it. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX) holds public companies accountable for internal controls over financial reporting, which pulls IT access and change controls into scope.
  • Contracts and industry standards: obligations can come from a contract (a service level agreement) or an industry body (PCI DSS) rather than from a law.

Ethics in artificial intelligence

Organizations now use AI to screen applicants, flag fraud, answer students, and triage alerts. The security leader is often the person asked whether a use is safe and fair. Five principles frame that answer.

  • Fairness and bias: a model trained on skewed data repeats the skew. Test outcomes across groups before and after deployment.
  • Transparency and explainability: people affected by a decision should know AI was involved and get a reason they can understand.
  • Accountability: a named owner answers for each AI system. The model never owns the decision.
  • Privacy: collect only the data the purpose needs, protect training data, and respect consent and retention rules.
  • Human oversight: keep a person in the loop for decisions with legal or significant effects, with the authority to override.

Two references help. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework is voluntary and organizes the work into four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage. The EU AI Act is law. It sorts AI systems by risk, bans a small set of unacceptable uses, and places the heaviest duties on high-risk systems such as those used in hiring or education.

💡 Exam TipWhen an AI question offers a technical fix and a governance answer, such as an impact assessment, a named owner, or human review, the governance answer is usually best. The ISC2 Code of Ethics applies to AI work as it does to any other.

Policy and Personnel Security

Policy turns governance into direction people can follow. The exam expects you to know the four document types and how they relate.

DocumentWhat it doesMandatory?Example
PolicyStates management’s intent and direction at a high level.Yes“All sensitive data is encrypted at rest.”
StandardSets a specific, measurable requirement that supports a policy.Yes“Use AES-256 for data at rest.”
ProcedureGives step-by-step instructions to meet a standard.Yes“How to enable disk encryption on a staff laptop.”
GuidelineOffers recommended practice where flexibility is acceptable.No“Tips for choosing a strong passphrase.”

A baseline is a minimum security configuration for a type of system. It is often treated alongside standards.

Personnel security across the employee life cycle

  • Screening and hiring: background and reference checks that match the sensitivity of the role.
  • Agreements: acceptable use policies, nondisclosure agreements, and noncompete clauses where the law allows them.
  • Onboarding, transfers, and termination: grant only the access a role needs, remove old access on transfer to prevent privilege creep, and revoke all access promptly when someone leaves.
  • Vendors, consultants, and contractors: the same controls by contract, service level agreements (SLAs) that set measurable security and availability commitments, monitoring, and the right to audit.
  • Administrative controls: separation of duties, job rotation, and mandatory vacations help detect and deter fraud.
💡 Exam TipFor an involuntary termination, disable access at the same time the person is notified, not afterward. Expect at least one question that tests the order of these steps.

Risk Management

Risk combines the likelihood that a threat will exploit a vulnerability with the impact if it does. Risk management finds, measures, and treats risk, then keeps watching it. The exam expects you to calculate, compare, and recommend.

Quantitative analysis

TermFormulaWorked example
Asset value (AV)Value of the assetStudent records system: $400,000
Exposure factor (EF)Percent of value lost in one eventRansomware event: 25%
Single loss expectancy (SLE)AV × EF$400,000 × 0.25 = $100,000
Annualized rate of occurrence (ARO)Expected events per yearOnce every five years: 0.2
Annualized loss expectancy (ALE)SLE × ARO$100,000 × 0.2 = $20,000
Safeguard valueALE before − ALE after − annual safeguard cost$20,000 − $5,000 − $8,000 = $7,000 per year

In the example, an $8,000 per year safeguard lowers the ARO to 0.05, so the ALE drops to $5,000. The safeguard saves $7,000 a year, so it is worth buying. If the result is negative, the control costs more than the risk it removes.

Qualitative analysis ranks risk with scales such as high, medium, and low, often in a likelihood and impact matrix. It is faster and captures harms that are hard to price, such as reputation. Most programs use both methods.

Risk responses

  • Mitigate (reduce): apply controls to lower likelihood or impact. Mitigation strategies range from technical controls to process changes, redundancy, and training, chosen by cost against benefit.
  • Transfer (share): shift the financial impact to another party, for example through cybersecurity insurance or a contract. Accountability does not transfer.
  • Avoid: stop the activity that creates the risk.
  • Accept: a documented management decision to retain a risk that falls within the appetite.

Ignoring a risk, sometimes called rejecting it, is never an acceptable response.

Control types and categories

By functionPreventive (stops an event), detective (finds it), corrective (fixes the damage), deterrent (discourages it), recovery (restores operations), compensating (an alternative when the primary control is not feasible), and directive (tells people what to do).
By categoryAdministrative or managerial (policy, training, screening), technical or logical (encryption, firewalls, access control lists), and physical (locks, fences, guards). Every control has both a function and a category.

Controls need assessment (do they work as designed?), continuous monitoring and measurement, and reporting to internal and external audiences. Maturity modeling supports continuous improvement: an organization rates each capability on a scale, such as initial, repeatable, defined, managed, and optimizing, and plans the next step up. Common frameworks for the process are NIST SP 800-37 (the Risk Management Framework), NIST SP 800-30 (risk assessment), and ISO 31000 with ISO/IEC 27005.

💡 Exam TipWatch for the trap answer that “transfers” responsibility. Insurance shares the cost of a loss. The organization remains accountable for protecting the data.

Continuity, Supply Chain, and Awareness

Business continuity requirements and the BIA

Domain 1 owns the requirements for business continuity. Domain 7 runs the plans and tests them. The business impact analysis is the starting point. It identifies critical functions and the systems, people, and external dependencies they rely on, then sets recovery targets.

  • Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD): the longest a function can be unavailable before the damage is unacceptable. Also called the maximum tolerable period of disruption.
  • Recovery time objective (RTO): the target time to restore the function. It must be shorter than the MTD.
  • Recovery point objective (RPO): the maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time. It drives how often you back up.
  • Work recovery time (WRT): time to verify data and resume work after systems return. RTO plus WRT must fit within the MTD.

Supply chain risk management

Products and services from suppliers can arrive tampered with, counterfeit, or carrying implants. A supplier can also bring reputational risk: a vendor breach or a scandal becomes your headline when your name is on the service. Mitigations include third-party assessment and monitoring, minimum security requirements in contracts, service level requirements, a silicon root of trust (an immutable hardware anchor that verifies firmware at boot), physically unclonable functions (hardware fingerprints that cannot be copied), and a software bill of materials (SBOM) that lists every component in a product.

Awareness, education, and training

Awareness changes attention, training builds skill for a role, and education builds understanding for a career. Effective programs use phishing simulations, security champions, and gamification, review content periodically for new trends such as AI and cryptocurrency, and measure effectiveness with results, not just attendance. Evaluation includes stakeholder engagement: leaders, managers, and staff help set goals, review results, and keep the program relevant.

💡 Exam TipMeasure an awareness program by behavior: lower phishing click rates and higher report rates. Completion percentages show reach, not effectiveness.

Threat Modeling

Threat modeling finds design-level threats before they become incidents. The usual steps are to decompose the system, often into a data flow diagram (DFD), identify threats to each element, rate them, and choose mitigations. The DFD uses four element types: external entities, processes, data stores, and data flows. Trust boundaries mark the places where data crosses from one level of trust to another. Most threats cluster at those boundaries.

STRIDE threatProperty violatedTypical mitigation
SpoofingAuthenticityStrong authentication, MFA, certificates
TamperingIntegrityHashes, digital signatures, MACs, access control
RepudiationNonrepudiationSecure audit logs, digital signatures, timestamps
Information disclosureConfidentialityEncryption, access control, data minimization
Denial of serviceAvailabilityRedundancy, rate limiting, filtering, capacity
Elevation of privilegeAuthorizationLeast privilege, input validation, sandboxing

Other methods

  • PASTA: the Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis, a seven-stage, risk-centric method that starts from business objectives.
  • Attack trees: a goal at the root and the ways to reach it as branches.
  • DREAD: an older scoring scheme (damage, reproducibility, exploitability, affected users, discoverability) used to rank threats.
  • MITRE ATT&CK: a knowledge base of real adversary tactics and techniques, useful to check a model against observed behavior.

    Faculty Teaching Segment

    Domain 1 is where many students first meet security as a business discipline. It lands best when students make decisions with numbers and owners, not just definitions.

    • Signature assignment: give teams a one-page profile of a fictional organization and three risks. Each team calculates ALE, recommends a response, names the risk owner, and writes a two-paragraph memo to leadership.
    • Rubric criteria: correct calculations, a response that fits the stated risk appetite, the right owner named, and plain language a dean or CFO could act on.
    • Threat modeling lab: students draw a DFD for a system they know, such as the learning management system or a campus app, then apply STRIDE per element as on the board above.
    • Course fit: this domain maps to an introductory security, governance, risk, and compliance (GRC), or policy course. It supports the CAE-CD Cybersecurity Planning and Management and Security Risk Analysis Knowledge Units.
    🎓 Teaching noteRun the STRIDE board twice: first individually, then in pairs who must agree before they check. The disagreements, especially over the audit log and the external entity, are where the learning happens.

    Scored Knowledge Check

    Test your understanding

    Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

    Q1.Before signing with a new cloud provider, a college reviews the provider’s SOC 2 Type II report and its breach history. Which concept does this BEST illustrate?

    Question 1 options
    Correct: B. Researching and verifying before a decision is due diligence. Due care would be acting on that knowledge, such as putting contract controls in place and operating them.

    Q2.A data warehouse is valued at $250,000. A flood would destroy 40% of its value, and floods are expected once every two years. What is the annualized loss expectancy?

    Question 2 options
    Correct: C. SLE = $250,000 × 0.40 = $100,000. ARO = 0.5 (once every two years). ALE = $100,000 × 0.5 = $50,000. Option A is the SLE, a common trap.

    Q3.An organization buys a cybersecurity insurance policy to cover breach response costs. Which statement is MOST accurate?

    Question 3 options
    Correct: A. Insurance is risk transfer, or sharing. It moves some of the cost of a loss, not the duty of care. Regulators and affected individuals still hold the organization accountable.

    Q4.A college plans to use an AI model to recommend which applicants are denied admission. What should the security leader recommend FIRST?

    Question 4 options
    Correct: D. A decision with significant effects on people calls for governance first: test for bias, assign accountability, and keep a human in the loop who can override. Encryption protects the data but does nothing for fairness, and accuracy alone does not prove a model is fair.

    Q5.A college runs a health clinic that bills an insurance plan for student visits. Which U.S. law MOST directly governs the clinic’s patient records?

    Question 5 options
    Correct: C. A provider that bills a health plan electronically is a HIPAA covered entity, so HIPAA protects its health information. The CFAA addresses computer crime, GDPR protects people in the EU, and PCI DSS is an industry standard for payment cards.

    Q6.During a BIA, the registrar says enrollment can be down for no more than 48 hours. Which value MUST be shorter than 48 hours?

    Question 6 options
    Correct: A. The 48 hours is the MTD. The RTO, plus the time to verify data and resume work, must fit inside it. The MTD is the limit, not a value set below it.

    Q7.A security manager discovers evidence that an employee sold student data. Leadership may refer the case to law enforcement. What should the manager do FIRST?

    Question 7 options
    Correct: D. A possible criminal case needs evidence handled properly from the start and the involvement of counsel. The other options can destroy evidence or compromise the case.

    Q8.A college worries that network switches from a new supplier could contain hidden hardware implants. Which control BEST addresses this supply chain risk?

    Question 8 options
    Correct: B. SCRM controls target the product and the supplier: trusted channels, hardware roots of trust that verify firmware, and independent assessment. Insurance and locks do not detect a compromised device.
