Day 2, Morning • Domain 2

Asset Security

You cannot protect what you have not found, and you cannot protect it well until you know what it is worth. Domain 2 follows information and assets from creation to destruction: who owns them, how they are classified and handled, how long they are kept, and how they are made unrecoverable at the end.

Session: Day 2, 9:00 to 11:00 Duration: ~2 hrs Exam Weight: 12% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Know it, own it, protect it, retire it

Asset security is the bridge between governance and technology. Domain 1 decides how much risk is acceptable. Domain 2 applies that decision to specific information and equipment. Classification tells people how much protection something needs. Ownership tells them who decides. Handling rules, retention schedules, and sanitization methods turn those decisions into daily practice.

This lesson covers classification and inventory, handling and provisioning, the data life cycle and data roles, retention and remanence, media sanitization, and the controls that protect data in each of its states.

🧭 Think like a managerThe owner decides; the custodian carries it out. When a question asks who classifies data, approves access, or accepts the risk for an information asset, the answer is the data owner, not the IT team that runs the system.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Classify information and assets by the impact of their loss, and explain how classification drives handling. Bloom: Apply
  • Distinguish the data roles: owner, controller, custodian, processor, and subject. Bloom: Understand
  • Select protection methods for data at rest, in transit, and in use, including DLP, DRM, and CASB. Bloom: Apply
  • Choose Clear, Purge, or Destroy for a given medium, sensitivity, and destination. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Apply scoping and tailoring to adapt a control baseline. Bloom: Analyze
  • Design a classroom data inventory and sanitization exercise with a rubric. Bloom: Create

Identify and Classify Information and Assets

Classification sorts information by the harm its loss would cause. The point is not the label. The point is that everyone handles a Restricted file the same careful way without having to ask. Classification also saves money, because low-value data does not need high-value controls.

U.S. governmentTypical commercialHarm if disclosed
Top SecretRestricted / ConfidentialExceptionally grave damage
SecretPrivate / SensitiveSerious damage
ConfidentialInternalDamage
UnclassifiedPublicLittle or none

Commercial labels vary by organization, so the exam tests the logic, not the words. Classification should be set by the owner, reviewed on a schedule, and lowered or raised when value changes, a step called declassification or reclassification.

Assets beyond data

  • Asset inventory: tangible assets (servers, laptops, phones, removable media, facilities) and intangible ones (data, software licenses, intellectual property, reputation). An accurate inventory is the precondition for every other control.
  • Asset classification: a system takes the classification of the most sensitive data it stores or processes.
  • Asset management: tracking each asset from acquisition through deployment, change, and disposal, usually in a configuration management database (CMDB).
  • Ownership: every asset has a named owner. An asset with no owner has no one to approve access or accept its risk.

Policies that hold it together

  • Data management policy: states how the organization classifies, labels, stores, shares, retains, and disposes of data, and who owns each decision. It is the document that makes classification enforceable rather than optional.
  • Asset management policy: sets the rules for the asset life cycle: what must be inventoried, who approves acquisition, how assets are tagged and tracked, how long they are kept, and how they are retired.
💡 Exam TipWhen a question asks what to do FIRST in a data protection program, look for inventory or classification. You cannot choose controls for data you have not found and valued.
2

Handling and Provisioning Assets Securely

Handling requirements tell people what each classification means in practice. They cover the whole path an asset travels.

  • Marking and labeling: marking is the human-readable classification on a document or screen. Labeling is often the physical or system tag, including metadata that tools such as DLP can read.
  • Storage: where each class may live (approved systems, locked cabinets, encrypted volumes) and who may hold copies.
  • Transmission and transport: encryption in transit, approved couriers, tamper-evident packaging, and logs of who handled media.
  • Provisioning: giving people and systems only the information and assets their role needs, recorded in the inventory, with ownership and access reviewed over time.
Least privilegeGrant the minimum access needed to perform a job. It limits what a compromised account can reach.
Need to knowEven with clearance, access to a specific item requires a business reason to see that item.

Physical and digital assets

Handling rules must cover every form an asset takes, because the same record can exist on paper, on a laptop, and in a cloud service at once.

  • Physical assets: printed records, removable media, and devices. Controls include clean desk rules, locked storage, sign-out logs, escorted transport, and approved destruction bins.
  • Digital or electronic assets: files, databases, messages, and backups. Controls include access control, classification labels in metadata, DLP, and logging.
  • Data encryption: encrypt at rest (full-disk, database, or file encryption) and in transit or motion (TLS, IPsec, SSH). Encryption is only as strong as key management: keys stored with the data protect very little.
  • Secure media destruction: media that held sensitive data leaves the organization only after approved sanitization or destruction, with a record. Section 4 covers the methods.
3

The Data Life Cycle and Data Roles

Data moves through a life cycle: create or collect, store, use, share, archive, and destroy. Each stage carries different risks, and each needs an owner’s decision about protection.

RoleResponsibilityCollege example
OwnerAccountable; classifies, approves access, sets retention.The registrar for student records
ControllerDecides purposes and means of processing personal data (privacy law).The college itself
CustodianOperates controls day to day.The database administrators
ProcessorProcesses personal data for the controller under contract.A cloud learning platform vendor
UserUses data under policy for authorized tasks.An advisor viewing transcripts
SubjectThe person the personal data describes.The student

Life cycle decisions

  • Collection: collect only what the purpose requires (data minimization) and tell subjects why.
  • Location: know where data is stored and processed, including cloud regions, because data location decides which laws apply.
  • Maintenance: keep data accurate and current, and keep copies in sync.
  • Retention and destruction: keep data as long as law and business need require, then destroy it on schedule.

4

Retention, Remanence, and Sanitization

Keeping data too briefly breaks the law. Keeping it too long creates risk with no benefit, because every extra record is one more thing to breach, disclose in litigation, or protect. A retention schedule sets the period for each record type, and legal holds suspend destruction when litigation is expected.

Assets have retention limits too. At end of life (EOL) the vendor stops selling a product. At end of support (EOS) it stops patching it. Systems past EOS need replacement, isolation, or compensating controls with documented risk acceptance.

Asset retention decides how long an asset stays in service and how long its records stay after it leaves. Asset retirement is the controlled exit: the owner approves it, data is migrated or archived under the retention schedule, the media is sanitized and verified, licenses and accounts are released, and only then is the asset removed from the inventory.

Remanence and NIST SP 800-88

Deleting a file removes the pointer, not the data. Formatting usually does the same. NIST SP 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization, defines three levels of sanitization.

LevelProtects againstExample methods
ClearSimple, noninvasive recovery with standard tools.Overwrite user-addressable storage; factory reset.
PurgeState-of-the-art laboratory recovery.Degaussing magnetic media; ATA Secure Erase or NVMe Sanitize; cryptographic erase.
DestroyRecovery of any kind; the media cannot be reused.Shredding, disintegration, pulverizing, melting, incineration.
  • Hard drives (magnetic): overwriting clears; degaussing purges but also ruins the drive.
  • Solid-state drives and flash: degaussing does nothing. Overwriting may miss spare and over-provisioned blocks managed by the controller. Use the drive’s sanitize command or cryptographic erase to purge, or destroy it to small particles.
  • Magnetic tape: degauss with a degausser rated for the tape’s coercivity, or destroy.
  • Paper: there is no clear or purge. Cross-cut shred, pulp, or incinerate.
  • Cloud storage: you cannot touch the provider’s disks. Cryptographic erase with keys you control, plus the provider’s contractual sanitization attestation, is the practical answer.

After sanitizing, verify the result on a sample and record a certificate of sanitization: who, what media, which method, and when.

⚠️ Common mistakeCryptographic erase only works if the media was encrypted from the start with a strong key that was never exposed. Encrypting a drive the day it is retired protects nothing that was written before.
5

Data Security Controls and Compliance

Controls follow the data through its three states.

StateMain risksTypical controls
At restTheft of media, unauthorized access to storesFull-disk and database encryption, access control, backups
In transitInterception, alterationTLS, IPsec, SSH, VPNs, message authentication
In useMemory scraping, screen capture, insider misuseTrusted execution environments, memory protection, session controls, screen masking

Choosing and adapting controls

  • Standards and controls selection: start from a recognized baseline, such as NIST SP 800-53, ISO/IEC 27002, or the CIS Controls, chosen to fit the organization’s obligations.
  • Scoping: remove controls that do not apply. A baseline wireless control does not apply to a system with no wireless interface.
  • Tailoring: adjust what remains, such as setting parameters or adding compensating controls, to match the mission and risk.

Data protection methods

  • Digital rights management (DRM): persistent controls that travel with a file, such as no printing, no forwarding, or expiry.
  • Data loss prevention (DLP): discovers sensitive content and blocks or alerts on movement through email, web, removable media, and cloud.
  • Cloud access security broker (CASB): sits between users and cloud services to give visibility, enforce policy, and find unsanctioned shadow IT.
  • Cloud-native application protection platform (CNAPP): one platform that combines cloud security posture management, workload protection, and identity and entitlement checks across cloud accounts. For asset security it finds exposed storage buckets, unencrypted databases, and sensitive data in places it should not be.

🔎 In practice

Three questions drive the answer: how sensitive was the data, where is the media going, and what does this medium physically allow? The same Confidential data needs a Clear for internal reuse, a Purge when a leased laptop goes back to the vendor, and Destroy for a flash drive that cannot be purged reliably. Whatever the level, the job is not done until someone verifies it and signs the certificate.

7

Faculty Teaching Segment

Asset security feels abstract until students hold the media and fill in the forms. Make it concrete with real artifacts from a fictional organization.

  • Signature assignment: give teams a data inventory of ten systems at a fictional college. For each, they name the owner and custodian, assign a classification with a one-line impact rationale, set a retention period, and choose a sanitization method for the media at end of life.
  • Rubric criteria: owner and custodian correctly separated, classification justified by impact rather than by system type, retention tied to a stated legal or business reason, and a sanitization method valid for the specific medium.
  • Lab idea: recover “deleted” files from a USB drive with a free forensic tool, then overwrite the drive and try again. Students see remanence for themselves. Use only drives and synthetic data you supply.
  • Course fit: this domain fits an introductory security, privacy, or GRC course. It supports the CAE-CD Privacy (PRI), Cybersecurity Planning and Management (CPM), and Policy, Legal, Ethics, and Compliance (PLE) Knowledge Units.
🎓 Teaching noteRun the Challenge mode as a pair activity and ask each pair to write one sentence for every scenario that names the physical reason behind the choice. “Flash memory ignores magnets” sticks far better than a memorized table.

Scored Knowledge Check

Test your understanding

Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

Q1.Who is responsible for determining the classification of the college’s student financial aid data?

Question 1 options
Correct: B. The data owner, a business manager accountable for the data, sets its classification. The DBA is a custodian, the CISO advises and runs the program, and students are data subjects.

Q2.A solid-state drive that stored Confidential data will be returned to a leasing company. Which method BEST sanitizes it?

Question 2 options
Correct: D. Media leaving organizational control with Confidential data needs a Purge. Firmware sanitize and cryptographic erase reach all flash blocks. Degaussing has no effect on flash, formatting is not sanitization, and an OS overwrite may miss over-provisioned blocks.

Q3.Under GDPR, which party decides the purposes and means of processing personal data?

Question 3 options
Correct: A. The controller determines why and how personal data is processed. A processor acts only on the controller’s instructions.

Q4.An organization adopts the NIST SP 800-53 moderate baseline and removes the controls for wireless access because the system has no wireless interface. What is this called?

Question 4 options
Correct: C. Scoping removes baseline controls that do not apply to the system. Tailoring adjusts the controls that remain to fit the organization.

Q5.A college runs workloads in three cloud accounts. The security team wants one tool that finds publicly exposed storage, unencrypted databases, and overly broad cloud permissions across all of them. Which tool BEST fits?

Question 5 options
Correct: A. A CNAPP combines posture management, workload protection, and entitlement checks across cloud accounts, which is exactly this need. DRM protects individual files, an on-premises IDS does not see cloud configuration, and laptop encryption protects endpoints.

Q6.Faculty are uploading course rosters to personal file-sharing services the college has not approved. Which tool BEST gives visibility and policy enforcement over this cloud use?

Question 6 options
Correct: C. A CASB sits between users and cloud services, discovers unsanctioned shadow IT, and enforces policy. DRM protects individual files but does not reveal which services are in use.

Q7.A department is retiring forty desktops that stored student records. Which step should happen LAST?

Question 7 options
Correct: D. The inventory entry is the evidence trail, so it closes only after approval, archiving, and verified sanitization. Removing assets first leaves no record of what happened to the data they held.

Q8.What is the MOST effective way to make sure staff handle a printed report according to its classification?

Question 8 options
Correct: B. Handling depends on people knowing the classification. Clear marking, supported by policy and training, tells every reader what to do. A cabinet protects one copy, and file encryption does nothing for the printout.
