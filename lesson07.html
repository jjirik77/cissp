Day 4, Afternoon • Domain 7

Security Operations

Domain 7 is where plans meet the night shift. It covers how an organization watches its systems, investigates what goes wrong, responds to incidents, and recovers from disasters. It is the widest domain on the exam. The common thread is discipline: follow the process, protect the evidence, and keep people safe first.

Session: Day 4, 1:00 to 5:00 Duration: ~3.5 hrs Exam Weight: 12.8% Knowledge Check: 8 Questions

Overview

Run it, watch it, recover it

Security operations is the daily work of keeping controls running. It includes investigations and forensics, logging and monitoring, configuration and change management, privileged access, incident response, patching, backup and recovery, disaster recovery testing, physical security, and personnel safety. Domain 1 sets the requirements. Domain 7 carries them out and proves they work.

This lesson moves from detection to recovery: how evidence is handled, how incidents run from detection to lessons learned, which preventive and detective tools matter, and how backup schemes and recovery sites meet recovery objectives.

🧭 Think like a managerHuman safety always comes first. After that, follow the documented process: the incident response plan, the change process, the evidence procedure. An answer that skips the process to act faster is usually wrong, even when the action itself sounds sensible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Apply evidence handling rules: chain of custody, order of volatility, and admissibility. Bloom: Apply
  • Sequence the phases of incident management from detection to lessons learned. Bloom: Understand
  • Select detective and preventive measures, including EDR, XDR, and SOAR, for a stated threat. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Compare full, incremental, and differential backups, and explain why ransomware resilience needs an immutable or offline copy. Bloom: Analyze
  • Choose a recovery site and a DR test type that fit the RTO, RPO, and budget. Bloom: Evaluate
  • Design a classroom tabletop exercise with injects and a debrief. Bloom: Create

Investigations and Evidence

Lesson 1 named the investigation types. Domain 7 covers how investigations are run. The goal is evidence that is reliable enough for the strictest likely audience, which may be a court.

  • Evidence collection and handling: identify, preserve, collect, examine, analyze, and present. Work on verified copies, not originals. Hash the original and the image to prove they match.
  • Chain of custody: record every transfer of the evidence, with names, times, and purpose. A gap can make evidence inadmissible.
  • Order of volatility: collect memory and live network state before pulling the power. Once a system is off, what was in RAM is gone.
  • Admissibility: evidence must be relevant, reliable, and legally obtained. Best evidence is the original or a verified duplicate. Hearsay rules can apply to some records; regularly kept business records are often an exception.
  • Investigative techniques: interviews, log and timeline analysis, and forensic examination of media, memory, networks, and software.
  • Artifacts: data files, computer artifacts such as registry entries and browser history, network captures and flow records, and mobile device data.
  • Reporting and documentation: record every step, tool, and finding so another examiner could repeat the work and reach the same result.
💡 Exam TipUse a write blocker when imaging storage, and verify the image with a hash. If an answer has the investigator browse the original drive to “take a quick look,” it is wrong.

Logging, Monitoring, and Core Concepts

You cannot respond to what you cannot see. Monitoring turns raw events into decisions, and it needs both good data and people who look at it.

  • Intrusion detection and prevention (IDPS): signature-based detection matches known patterns; anomaly-based detection flags departures from a baseline. An IDS alerts; an IPS sits inline and can block.
  • SIEM: centralizes and correlates logs. It depends on accurate time synchronization across sources.
  • SOAR: security orchestration, automation, and response. It connects the SIEM, ticketing, endpoint, identity, and firewall tools, and runs playbooks: enrich an alert with threat intelligence, open a case, disable an account, or isolate a host. Automation handles routine, well-understood steps in seconds; analysts approve the high-impact ones.
  • Continuous monitoring and tuning: adjust rules to cut false positives without creating false negatives.
  • Egress monitoring: watch outbound traffic for data leaving the organization. Data loss prevention (DLP) inspects email, web uploads, cloud sharing, and removable media for sensitive content, such as student ID numbers, and can alert, encrypt, or block. Unusual volume and destinations still matter, because encrypted exfiltration hides its content.
  • Log management: collect, protect from alteration, retain per policy and law, and review.
  • Threat intelligence: feeds of indicators and adversary behavior, plus threat hunting, which searches proactively for attackers already inside.
  • UEBA: behavior baselines for users and devices that expose insider threats and stolen credentials.

Foundational operations concepts

ConceptWhat it meansWhy it matters
Need to know and least privilegeAccess only to the data and rights a task requiresLimits damage from mistakes and compromise
Separation of dutiesNo single person controls a whole sensitive processRequires collusion to commit fraud
Privileged account managementVaulted, monitored, time-limited administrator accessAdministrator accounts are the top target
Job rotationStaff move between roles on a scheduleDetects fraud and builds backup skills
Defense in depthLayered, independent controls so one failure does not expose the assetAn attacker must defeat several controls, and each layer is a chance to detect

Configuration management keeps systems in a known state. Provision from approved images, set a secure baseline, automate deployment and drift detection, and track every system in a configuration management database. Resource protection covers media management: label, store, track, and sanitize media according to the classification of the data on it.

Media protection techniques

  • Archiving: move data that must be kept but is rarely used to protected long-term storage, with an index, an integrity check, and a retention date.
  • Encryption: encrypt media at rest so a lost tape or drive exposes nothing, and manage the keys separately from the media.
  • Disposal and reuse: sanitize before reuse or disposal, using the clear, purge, or destroy decision from Lesson 2, and keep a record of each item.
  • Data states: protect data at rest (encryption, access control), in transit (TLS, VPN), and in use (memory protection, restricted sessions, confidential computing).

Incident Management

An event is any observable occurrence. An incident is an event that harms or threatens confidentiality, integrity, or availability. The exam uses a seven-step model.

PhaseWhat happens
1. DetectionAlerts, reports, and monitoring identify a possible incident; the team confirms and declares it.
2. ResponseActivate the incident response team and plan; begin collecting evidence.
3. MitigationContain the damage, for example by isolating a host, without destroying evidence.
4. ReportingInform management and, where required, regulators, partners, and affected people.
5. RecoveryRestore systems to normal operation from known-good sources.
6. RemediationFix the root cause so the incident cannot repeat.
7. Lessons learnedReview what happened and improve the plan, controls, and training.

Reporting is shown as one step, but it continues throughout. Legal deadlines for breach notification may start the clock early.

💡 Exam TipContainment comes before eradication and recovery. If an answer restores a server from backup before the attacker’s access path is closed, the attacker can simply return.

Prevention, Patching, and Change

Detective and preventive measures

  • Firewalls: network firewalls filter by address, port, and state; next-generation firewalls add application awareness and intrusion prevention; web application firewalls inspect HTTP traffic for attacks such as injection.
  • Allowlist and denylist: an allowlist permits only approved software or destinations and is stronger; a denylist blocks known bad items and is easier to bypass.
  • Third-party security services: a managed security service provider (MSSP) monitors and responds around the clock for a small team, and a DDoS scrubbing service reroutes traffic through its own network during an attack, filters the flood, and passes clean traffic back. Both run under contract and service levels.
  • Sandboxing: runs untrusted code in isolation to observe what it does.
  • Honeypots and honeynets: decoy systems that attract attackers and reveal their methods. Use them for enticement, never entrapment.
  • Endpoint protection: anti-malware uses signature, heuristic, and behavior-based detection. Endpoint detection and response (EDR) records process, file, and network activity on each device so analysts can investigate and isolate a host remotely. Extended detection and response (XDR) correlates that data with email, identity, network, and cloud signals for a single view of an attack.
  • Machine learning and AI tools: help find anomalies at scale, but need tuning, oversight, and human review.

Patch, vulnerability-based, and change management

Patch management inventories systems, evaluates patches, tests them, approves them through change management, deploys them, and verifies the result. Vulnerability-based management goes further than patching on a calendar: it scans, prioritizes each weakness by severity, exploitation in the wild, and asset value, then tracks remediation to closure. A flaw on an internet-facing server that attackers are already using jumps the queue. Change management requires a request, an impact assessment, approval by a change advisory board, a tested rollback plan, implementation in a window, and documentation. Emergency changes still follow a defined process, with review after the fact.

Recovery Strategies

Backup types

TypeCopiesBackup timeRestore needs
FullEverythingLongestThe latest full only
IncrementalChanges since the last backup of any typeShortestThe last full plus every incremental since, in order
DifferentialChanges since the last fullGrows each dayThe last full plus the latest differential

Backup storage strategies include onsite copies for fast restores, offsite copies to survive a site loss, and cloud storage. A common rule of thumb is three copies, on two types of media, with one offsite.

  • Immutable backups: write once, read many storage, such as cloud object lock, that no one can change or delete until a retention period ends, even with administrator credentials. Ransomware operators target backups first, so at least one copy must be immutable or offline.
  • Encrypted backups: backups hold everything, so encrypt them in transit and at rest and keep the keys apart from the backup system. A backup you cannot decrypt is no backup at all.

Recovery sites

  • Hot site: fully equipped and close to current; ready in minutes to hours; the most expensive.
  • Warm site: power, network, and some equipment; data must be restored; ready in hours to a few days.
  • Cold site: space, power, and cooling only; equipment must be brought in; ready in days to weeks; the cheapest.
  • Mobile site: a trailer or container data center moved where needed.
  • Reciprocal agreement: two organizations agree to host each other; cheap but hard to enforce, and capacity is rarely there. Resource capacity agreements formalize this.
  • Cloud recovery: systems are rebuilt from templates and images in a cloud region, paying mainly when used.
  • Multiple processing sites: active-active centers that share the load, so a site loss causes little or no outage.

Resilience and recovery metrics

High availability keeps a service running through component failures with clustering and failover. Fault tolerance continues with no interruption, for example RAID 1 mirroring, RAID 5 striping with parity, or RAID 6 with two parity blocks. RAID 0 stripes for speed and offers no redundancy. Quality of service prioritizes critical traffic so it keeps working under load.

Measure recovery against a baseline of normal performance. Mean time between failures (MTBF) is the average time a component runs before it fails, so a higher value means more reliable equipment. Mean time to resolve (MTTR) is the average time from failure or detection until service is fully restored, so a lower value means faster recovery. Track both over time: MTBF guides what to replace or make redundant, and MTTR shows whether DR processes and practice are working.

💡 Exam TipRAID and clustering protect against hardware failure, not against deletion, corruption, or ransomware, because they copy the damage instantly. Only backups protect against those.
💡 Exam TipIf an attacker with domain administrator rights could delete every backup, the backups are part of the blast radius. The BEST answer for ransomware resilience is an immutable or offline copy that those credentials cannot reach.

DR Testing, BC, and Safety

A disaster recovery plan covers response, personnel, communications, assessment, restoration, training and awareness, lessons learned, and metrics. Communications need both methods and prioritization: who hears first, in what order, and through which channel if email and phones are down. Safety and the response team come first, then leadership, then staff, customers, and regulators as required. A plan is only as good as its last test.

DR testWhat happensDisruption risk
Read-through (checklist)Team members review the plan on their own and report problemsNone
Walkthrough (tabletop)The team meets and talks through a scenario step by stepNone
SimulationA realistic scenario is acted out, up to but not including moving operationsVery low
ParallelThe recovery site runs real work while the primary site keeps runningLow
Full interruptionThe primary site is shut down and operations move to the recovery siteHigh; needs senior approval

Business continuity planning exercises extend the same idea to whole business processes. Plan both internal communications, to staff and leadership, and external communications, to students, partners, the media, and regulators where needed, and rehearse who may speak for the organization.

Physical security and personnel safety

  • External security controls: fencing, lighting, gates, bollards, landscaping that removes hiding places, and cameras that cover approaches and parking.
  • Internal security controls: badges, mantraps, locks, guards, visitor escorts, and zones that tighten as you move toward sensitive rooms.
  • Travel: loaner devices, VPN use, and briefings for high-risk destinations.
  • Security training and awareness: insider threats, social media exposure, and MFA fatigue, where attackers flood a user with push prompts.
  • Emergency management: evacuation, shelter in place, and accountability for every person.
  • Duress: codes or alarms that let a person signal they are being forced, without alerting the attacker.
💡 Exam TipIn any emergency question, the first priority is the safety of people. Data, systems, and evidence come after.

      🔎 In practice

      Try a Saturday failure with incremental backups: six sets must be restored in order, and losing any one breaks the chain. Switch to differential and the restore needs only two sets, but each night’s backup grows. Then try the payments scenario. No nightly scheme meets a one-hour RPO, because data written since 11:00 p.m. is gone. Tight RPOs call for replication or journaling, not better tapes. Finally, set the failure to ransomware. Online and replicated backups are encrypted along with the servers, so there is nothing to restore. Only the immutable or offline copy survives.

      Faculty Teaching Segment

      Students often learn operations as a list of tools. It lands better as a sequence of decisions under time pressure, with evidence and people to protect.

      • Signature assignment: run a 50-minute tabletop on a ransomware scenario at a fictional college. Release injects every 10 minutes: the first alert, a ransom note, a reporter’s call, a failed restore, and a regulator’s question. Teams record each decision and who owns it.
      • Rubric criteria: phases in the right order, containment before recovery, evidence preserved with a chain of custody, required notifications identified, and lessons learned with named owners and dates.
      • Lab idea: students image a small virtual disk in an isolated lab, hash the original and the image, document the chain of custody, and then use the planner above to write a backup and recovery plan that meets a given RTO and RPO.
      • Course fit: this domain maps to security operations, digital forensics, and business continuity courses. It supports the CAE-CD Network Defense (NDF), Digital Forensics (DFS), and Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery (BCD) Knowledge Units.
      🎓 Teaching noteAsk students to find the cheapest plan in the planner that still meets every objective for enrollment. The answer forces a trade between site cost and restore effort, which is exactly the conversation a CISO has with a CFO.

      Scored Knowledge Check

      Test your understanding

      Select the best answer for each question, then submit for your score and the rationale for every item. Like the exam, several options may be true; choose the best one.

      Q1.An investigator arrives at a running server that may hold evidence of a crime. Which evidence should be collected FIRST?

      Question 1 options
      Correct: A. Following the order of volatility, memory is lost when the system powers off, so it is captured before the disk. Backups and documentation are the least volatile.

      Q2.An organization takes a full backup Sunday night and incremental backups Monday through Friday nights. The server fails Saturday morning. What must be restored?

      Question 2 options
      Correct: C. Each incremental holds only the changes since the previous backup, so all of them are needed after the full, in order. Option B describes a differential restore.

      Q3.A college needs a recovery site that can be running within two days and wants to keep costs moderate. Which option fits BEST?

      Question 3 options
      Correct: B. A warm site has power, network, and some equipment, and is usually ready in hours to a few days at moderate cost. A hot site exceeds the need at higher cost; a cold site takes too long; reciprocal agreements are hard to enforce.

      Q4.Leadership wants to confirm the recovery site can process real transactions, but will not accept any risk of an outage at the primary site. Which test should be used?

      Question 4 options
      Correct: D. A parallel test runs real work at the recovery site while the primary keeps running. A full interruption risks an outage; read-through and tabletop tests do not process real transactions.

      Q5.A security team detects ransomware spreading across file servers. After declaring an incident, what should the team do NEXT?

      Question 5 options
      Correct: D. Mitigation, or containment, comes before recovery. Restoring before the infection is contained lets it encrypt the restored data again. Lessons learned come last.

      Q6.A college keeps nightly backups on a disk array and replicates them to a second campus. A ransomware group with domain administrator credentials encrypted both copies. Which change would BEST have preserved a usable backup?

      Question 6 options
      Correct: B. Any copy the stolen credentials can reach can be encrypted or deleted. An immutable copy, such as one under object lock, or an offline copy cannot be altered from the network. More replicas, a different backup type, and RAID all copy the damage.

      Q7.Analysts in a two-person SOC spend most of each shift looking up the same threat intelligence for phishing alerts and opening tickets by hand. Mean time to resolve is rising. Which investment BEST addresses the problem?

      Question 7 options
      Correct: A. The bottleneck is repetitive response work, which orchestration and automation remove. More logs or lower thresholds add alerts without adding capacity, and a honeynet gathers intelligence but does not speed triage.

      Q8.A fire alarm sounds in the data center during a forensic acquisition. What should the investigator do FIRST?

      Question 8 options
      Correct: C. Human safety is always the first priority. Evidence and systems matter, but never more than people.
